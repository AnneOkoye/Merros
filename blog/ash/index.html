The Ash: The World Tree That's Fighting for Its Own Survival Right Now

5 min read

In Norse mythology, Yggdrasil is the tree that holds the entire universe together. Its branches reach into the heavens, its roots stretch into the underworld, and the nine worlds of Norse cosmology, gods, giants, humans, the dead, all rest somewhere among its structure. Both the Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson's thirteenth-century Prose Edda describe it the same way: a giant ash tree, standing at the exact center of everything that exists.

The scholar E. O. James, in his 1966 study The Tree of Life, called Yggdrasil "the Cosmic tree par excellence," and the description holds up against comparable myths worldwide. World-tree stories appear independently across the Americas, Asia, Africa, and the Middle East, but the Norse version is unusually specific about its species, and unusually central to the mythology built around it. Odin hung from Yggdrasil for nine days and nights, deliberately, to gain knowledge of the runes. The three Norns tend the tree with water from a sacred well so its branches do not decay. When Yggdrasil trembles, in the mythology, that tremor signals the arrival of Ragnarok, the end of the world itself.

It is, by any measure, one of the heaviest symbolic loads any tree species has ever been asked to carry.

Right now, ash trees are dying by the billions

Here is the part of this story that has nothing to do with mythology. In 2002, an invasive beetle called the emerald ash borer was discovered in the Detroit area, almost certainly arriving in wood packaging material shipped from Asia. It has since killed tens of millions of ash trees across the United States and Canada, with over 40 million dead in Michigan alone, and confirmed infestations now spread across 38 states and the District of Columbia.

The insect's larvae are the actual killers. They tunnel beneath the bark into the water-conducting tissue, cutting off the tree's ability to move water and nutrients between roots and canopy. Detection is genuinely difficult: an infested tree can carry the pest for up to four years before visible symptoms appear in the upper canopy, by which point the damage is usually irreversible. Once symptoms show, most infested trees die within two to four years without treatment.

North America is estimated to hold somewhere between seven and nine billion ash trees. Nobody currently knows how many will ultimately be lost. It is one of the more significant tree species collapses happening anywhere in the world right now, largely outside public attention.

A tree built to appear bare, on purpose

Ash trees flower before they leaf out each spring, which means for a specific window every year, an ash stands with visible reproductive structures on fully bare branches, no foliage yet to soften or hide the tree's actual skeletal shape. Botanically, ash also follows a strict opposite branching pattern: leaves, branches, and buds all emerge directly across from each other along the stem, one of the more reliable ways to identify an ash tree even at a distance, in any season, leaves or no leaves.

Ash trees are dioecious, meaning individual trees are either male or female, and only female trees go on to produce the tree's distinctive single-winged seed pods, called samaras, which cluster on the branches and often persist into winter long after the leaves themselves have dropped.

Why bat makers and toolmakers both reach for ash

Long before anyone worried about the emerald ash borer, woodworkers had already settled on ash for one specific reason: it absorbs shock better than almost any other hardwood without cracking. That property made it the standard choice for tool handles, axe handles, hammer handles, anything meant to be swung repeatedly and take repeated impact without transmitting all of that force straight into the user's hands.

Baseball carried this further than most other uses. Ash bats have been part of the sport for over a century, with players including Babe Ruth relying on them, and the wood's open, porous grain structure is exactly what lets it flex slightly on contact and absorb the shock of hitting a ball rather than simply breaking. That same open grain is also, notably, part of what made ash such easy prey for the emerald ash borer's larvae once the beetle arrived in North America: the very structure that gives the wood its useful give also made it comparatively easy for the insect to tunnel through.

There is something worth sitting with in that overlap. The same physical property, wood built to absorb repeated impact without shattering, both made ash indispensable to human tool use for centuries and left it structurally vulnerable to the specific threat now killing it by the billions. Usefulness and vulnerability, in this particular tree, turn out to come from exactly the same source.

Holding two truths about the same tree

It is a strange position for one species to occupy: mythologically central to an entire cosmology on one hand, and facing a genuine, currently unfolding survival crisis on the other. Both things are true about the ash tree at the exact same time, and neither cancels the other out.

If anything, the mythology reads differently once you know about the emerald ash borer. Yggdrasil's story was never really about permanence in the sense of nothing ever threatening it. The Norse myths are explicit that the tree faces real danger: it trembles before Ragnarok, it requires active tending from the Norns just to keep from decaying, and its survival is treated as something that has to be maintained, not something automatically guaranteed. A tree that stands bare before it stands full, that holds its position through a period genuinely marked by uncertainty and risk, was arguably always the more honest kind of world tree to have chosen.

What it means to choose the ash

This is not a tree whose meaning is about untouched stability, the way an oak's might be. Ash asks something closer to standing through the in-between: through the bare branches before the leaves come, through flowering before there is any foliage to protect it, and now, as it happens, through a genuine fight for the species' own continued existence. Choosing it means choosing to hold your position through a period where the outcome is not yet settled, because a real season of visible vulnerability, not just resilience, is part of what the tree has always represented.
