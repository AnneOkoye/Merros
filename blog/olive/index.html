The Olive: A Tree Old Enough to Have Watched Empires Rise and Fall

5 min read

On Crete, in the village of Ano Vouves, there is an olive tree that was already ancient when Alexander the Great was alive. Estimates place its age somewhere between 2,000 and 4,000 years, which means it may predate the Parthenon. It still produces fruit. In 2004, branches cut from it were woven into the victors' wreaths for that year's Athens Olympics, a tree older than most nations providing the crown for the modern world's most visible athletic competition.

How you actually age a tree that old

Determining the real age of an ancient olive tree is harder than counting rings, because olive wood often hollows out or splits with extreme age, destroying the earliest growth rings entirely. A 2020 study in the journal Dendrochronologia, working through the ScienceDirect archive, combined tree-ring analysis with radiocarbon dating to tackle this directly. Their findings complicate the popular narrative in an interesting way: most monumental olive trees, the ones with the largest trunks and most dramatic, twisted silhouettes, likely have maximum ages between 300 and 500 years, considerably younger than folklore often claims, even though a genuine handful of exceptional individuals, like the Vouves tree, do appear to be authentically ancient.

The researchers were not trying to debunk the tree's reputation. They were trying to separate size from age, since with olives, unlike with many other species, a wider trunk does not reliably indicate a proportionally older tree. Growing conditions, grafting history, and periodic regeneration from the root system all complicate the relationship between girth and true age.

A tree that can regenerate itself from the ground up

That regeneration ability is a large part of why some olive trees genuinely do reach extraordinary ages. Cut an olive tree down, even to the ground, and new shoots will often sprout directly from its root system, allowing what is functionally the same organism to continue growing for additional centuries. This is not the same as an unbroken trunk surviving intact for four thousand years. It is closer to a lineage that keeps rebuilding itself from the same root base, generation after generation, in the same spot.

The tree's biology supports this kind of longevity in more ordinary ways too. Olive trees are evergreen, native to the seasonal drought conditions of the Mediterranean basin, and domesticated so long ago that fossil evidence places wild olive populations in the eastern Mediterranean over 20 million years before human civilization existed to cultivate them.

Peace, wisdom, and a dove that returned with a branch

The olive branch's association with peace traces back at least as far as the biblical flood narrative, where a dove returns to Noah's ark carrying an olive branch as the first sign that the waters were receding and land had reappeared. That single image did enormous cultural work: the olive branch remains a peace symbol across cultures that otherwise share very little religious or historical overlap, still invoked today in entirely secular, political contexts far removed from its original source.

In ancient Greece, olive oil and olive wreaths carried their own separate weight, tied to athletic and civic honor well before the modern Olympics revived the tradition. Vincent van Gogh, working in Provence centuries later, painted the region's olive groves repeatedly, drawn specifically to what he described as a kind of spiritual force he located in their gnarled, twisting forms, brushwork that tried to make the soil and sky feel as animated as the trees themselves.

Where the domesticated olive actually came from

Genetics has settled a question that folklore alone could never resolve: where and when humans first domesticated the wild olive. A 2013 study in Proceedings of the Royal Society B, tracing the "complex history of the olive tree," and later genomic work published through BMC Biology and New Phytologist, converge on a specific answer. The cultivated olive was domesticated from its wild ancestor, known as the oleaster, roughly 6,000 years ago, in the northern Levant, an area spanning parts of modern Turkey and Syria.

From that single point of origin, cultivation spread westward across the Mediterranean through successive waves of Phoenician, Greek, and Roman expansion, with cultivated trees repeatedly cross-pollinating with wild populations they encountered along the way. Genomic research has found that this was not a one-time event followed by pure inheritance. Instead, cultivated olive lineages show recurring genetic admixture with wild trees across the Mediterranean basin, meaning the domesticated olive growing in a Spanish grove today still carries a measurable genetic imprint from countless local wild trees it interbred with over six thousand years, not one clean, unbroken lineage traced back to a single ancestor tree.

This matters more than it might first appear. It means "the olive tree" is not really a fixed thing handed down unchanged since the Neolithic. It is closer to an ongoing, still-active conversation between cultivated and wild populations, one that has been running continuously since long before anyone was writing down its terms.

What actually shapes that gnarled trunk

The twisted, knotted appearance so associated with old olive trees is not decorative. It is the visible record of a very long life spent responding to real stress: drought years, disease, human pruning across generations, occasional fire. Each hardship leaves the trunk slightly more asymmetrical than it started, and the tree simply continues growing around its own accumulated history rather than trying to correct it.

That is, in the end, a fairly direct description of what it means to choose this tree. Not smoothness. Not an unmarked trunk. A shape that openly carries what it has been through, still standing, still producing fruit, centuries after most of what it witnessed has already been forgotten by everyone else.

Sources referenced. Dendrochronologia (ScienceDirect), "Demystifying the age of old olive trees" (2020); Forbes, "Meet The 4,000-Year-Old Olive Tree That Saw The Rise And Fall Of Alexander The Great's Empire," reporting on the Vouves tree.
