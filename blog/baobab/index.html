The Baobab: The Tree That Stores 30,000 Gallons of Water Inside Itself

4 min read

A large baobab can hold up to 120,000 liters of water in its trunk. That is somewhere north of 30,000 gallons, stored not in a hollow chamber but inside spongy, fibrous wood that works something like a natural sponge, soaking up moisture during the rains and releasing it slowly over the many dry months that follow.

Elephants have figured this out too. In parts of East Africa, they strip bark from baobab trunks specifically to reach the moisture underneath, especially during drought. It is one of the few relationships in nature where a tree functions less like scenery and more like infrastructure.

An upside-down tree, and a good reason for it

Baobabs are leafless for most of the year. They only grow foliage during the rainy season, typically two to four months out of twelve, and for the rest of the year their bare, thick branches spread out from a swollen trunk in a way that has struck people across many cultures as unmistakably root-like. African folklore across multiple regions arrived independently at the same image: a god, annoyed or careless, planted the tree upside down, so what looks like roots reaching into the sky is really the crown.

The science underneath that legend is more mundane but no less remarkable. Dropping leaves for most of the year is a direct water-saving strategy: fewer leaves means less surface area for water to evaporate through. A 2006 study of baobab water relations during the rainy season found that the trees can reduce their transpiration rate, water lost through leaf pores, by up to 90 percent compared to species like Ziziphus mauritiana, which manages only about a 25 percent reduction under the same conditions. That gap is not a small difference in degree. It is closer to a different survival strategy entirely.

Built to outlast droughts, and centuries

The baobab's trunk diameter is often more remarkable than its height. While a mature baobab typically tops out around 65 feet, its trunk can measure 36 to 46 feet across, a proportion that looks almost architectural rather than organic. Botanists describe this growth form as pachycaul: a body built wide and squat rather than tall and narrow, optimized for water storage over vertical competition for sunlight.

That storage capacity is directly tied to the tree's extraordinary lifespan. Some individual baobabs are estimated to be several thousand years old, predating most of recorded human history in the regions where they grow. The tree evolved during the Cretaceous period, tens of millions of years before humans existed to name it, and it still occupies 32 African countries today, along with populations in Madagascar and parts of Australia.

Why "tree of life" is not just a nickname

The name is earned on practical grounds, not sentiment. Baobab fruit pulp carries roughly six times the vitamin C of an orange by some measurements, and it is sold across East African markets in both raw and powdered form. The leaves are edible. The bark has historically been processed into rope and woven textiles. The hollow trunks of very old baobabs, once the heartwood has decayed away, have served as shelters, storage rooms, and even small trading posts, since the living outer wood can continue growing around an empty core without harm to the tree.

For wildlife, the baobab often functions as the only reliable water and food source across long stretches of dry season, making it something closer to a keystone piece of infrastructure for an entire ecosystem than an ordinary tree among many.

Not one species, but nine

"Baobab" is not a single tree so much as a family name. The genus Adansonia comprises nine recognized species, and their distribution is oddly lopsided: six are found only on Madagascar, two occupy mainland Africa and the Arabian Peninsula, and just one, Adansonia gregorii, grows natively in northwestern Australia, separated from its African relatives by an entire ocean. A 2024 genomic study published in Nature sequenced all eight then-recognized species and argued that Madagascar, not mainland Africa, should be considered the genus's actual center of origin, a conclusion that reorders the assumption most people carry about where baobabs "really" belong.

Three of the six Malagasy species are now listed as threatened, two of them critically so, largely due to shrinking habitat. Researchers modeling climate change's effect on the island's baobab populations project that one widespread species could lose over 40 percent of its currently suitable habitat, which means a genus that has already survived tens of millions of years of Earth's climate history is now facing a threat on a timescale it has never had to negotiate before: a handful of decades, not eons.

What it means to choose the baobab

Pine holds steady through cold by staying exactly the same all winter. Willow survives storms by giving way completely in the moment. The baobab does neither. It survives by preparing months in advance, storing enormous reserves during the good season specifically so it has something to draw on later, when conditions turn hard and stay hard for a long time.

That is a specific kind of resilience, and it is not the flashy kind. Nobody watches a baobab bend dramatically in a storm. What they watch, instead, is a tree that quietly outlasted a drought that killed everything smaller around it, because it did the unglamorous work of stocking up months before anyone needed to.
